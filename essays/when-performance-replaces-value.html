When Performance Replaces Value

Jayface • Published: 2026. October 5.

The Cultural Cost of Short-Term Measurement

If a cultural product is given only a short time to prove itself, we are not only discarding weaker works quickly. We are also depriving good works of the time they may need to become truly significant. And this goes far beyond music. The same logic increasingly affects films, visual culture, books, and even ideas. We have gradually become accustomed to judging cultural value through measurable, short-term performance.

But performance can be measured. Value cannot necessarily be measured in the same way.

The idea that “if it does not work now, it is not good enough” is a dangerous cultural measure. But what does “not working” actually mean? Did it fail to receive enough algorithmic recommendations? Did too few people click on it, at a particular and constantly changing moment? Did it fail to enter the right playlist? Was its release week unfortunate? Did it receive too little activity during its first days? Did it fail to fit the current trend? Or did it simply lack the financial and promotional resources needed to remain visible?

This last question is particularly important for independent artists. In practice, not every creator has an equal opportunity to keep a work in the public eye for weeks or months. Larger and better-funded labels can invest in promotion, visibility, and sustained attention in ways that are often simply unavailable to independent creators. Yet the resulting performance can still be interpreted as if it were a neutral measure of the work itself. We are trying to measure cultural value through a competition in which the participants do not even begin on equal terms.

None of these things are the same as determining whether a work is good or bad.

There is an ironic contradiction here. The more we try to determine cultural value through short-term indicators, the more we reshape the cultural environment itself around the things that can succeed immediately. What performs quickly receives attention, while what needs time is increasingly pushed aside. But some works do not reveal their full significance immediately. They may need repeated encounters, changing circumstances, personal experiences, or simply the passage of time before their value becomes apparent.

If a cultural product is given only a short period in which to prove itself, we are not simply filtering out what is weak. We may also be preventing something valuable from having the time it needs to become important.
