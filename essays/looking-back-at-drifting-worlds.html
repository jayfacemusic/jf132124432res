Looking Back at Drifting Worlds

Jayface • Published: 2026. October 8.

On Music, Memory, and the Worlds I Create

An album is a strange thing. When it is released, it belongs to the present. It is new, immediate, and surrounded by the circumstances of its arrival. There is a release date, a cover, a tracklist, announcements, descriptions, and the anticipation of people hearing the music for the first time. But eventually, that moment disappears. The album remains, yet the circumstances surrounding it begin to fade. What was once new becomes part of the past. The music no longer arrives as an event. It remains as a trace of the time in which it was created. A year after the release of ‘Drifting Worlds’, I find myself looking at the album differently. Not as a new release, and not as something that needs to be introduced, but as something that has already become part of my own musical history.

‘Drifting Worlds’ was released in 2025 as my fourth album, independently through my own label, Jayface Music. I wrote and produced the album myself, and its visual identity was also developed as part of the same creative process. Looking back now, I realize that the album was never really about telling one continuous story. It was more about moving between worlds.

From One World to Another

The title ‘Drifting Worlds’ was created from two of the tracks themselves: ‘Drift’ and ‘Worlds’. Both tracks had already emerged independently, each carrying its own atmosphere, idea, and creative intention. When I brought the ten tracks together as an album, I deliberately chose these two titles and combined them to create ‘Drifting Worlds’.

The choice was not accidental. ‘Drift’ suggested movement, while ‘Worlds’ suggested the different places and atmospheres that existed across the album. Bringing the two together created a title that represented the relationship between them: movement between worlds.

Drifting implies movement without necessarily having a fixed destination. Worlds suggests places that are distinct from one another, each with its own atmosphere and character. Together, they describe something that feels increasingly familiar to me about music itself: we do not always move through one continuous landscape. Sometimes we move from one emotional or imagined world to another.

The album begins with ‘The Tree Of Life: Beginnings’, a melodic progressive trance track inspired by nature, life, and the idea of beginnings. It is followed by ‘The Green Valley’, a brighter and more uplifting interpretation of natural beauty. Then the landscape changes. ‘Back To The Desert’ leaves behind the greenery and moves into heat, emptiness, and an almost hostile environment. There is no water and no shade. The contrast is intentional. The album does not need every track to exist in the same emotional climate.

Perhaps that is one of the reasons I have always been drawn to electronic music. Music can create places without physically describing them. A melody can suggest distance. A rhythm can suggest movement. A texture can feel cold, warm, mechanical, organic, familiar, or completely alien. None of these places have to exist outside the listener's imagination.

Drifting

The track ‘Drift’ sits somewhere between these worlds. Its inspiration came from drifting - the physical act of spinning, turning, and maintaining movement in a controlled state of instability. There is something strangely appropriate about that idea in relation to an album. Music itself is a form of controlled movement. A track begins somewhere, develops, changes direction, and eventually disappears. Yet while it is playing, it creates the illusion of somewhere else. Perhaps this is what an album ultimately does. It allows us to drift. Not necessarily away from reality, but temporarily outside the ordinary structure of it.

Returning to the Past

Not everything on Drifting Worlds was created as something completely new. ‘Pure Love Remix’ and ‘Ashes In The Wind Remix’ both return to earlier material. That makes them different from the newer compositions around them. A remix is not simply another version of a song. It is also a conversation with something that already exists. The original Pure Love had been released a year earlier. Ashes In The Wind reached further back, to 2022.

Time has passed. Taste changes, experience accumulates, and production techniques evolve. Returning to an older track means approaching familiar material from a different point in time. A remix becomes more than a technical reconstruction. It becomes a way of revisiting the past through the perspective of the present. It becomes a form of memory. The past is not reproduced exactly. It is interpreted again.

Worlds

The track ‘Worlds’ gave the album its other defining word. It is one of the heavier and more hybrid pieces on the record, combining progressive, techno, and trance influences in a way that belongs to the broader musical language I have developed over time. Placed within the album, however, its title acquires another meaning. There are many worlds inside an album. There is the world of the producer who created it. There is the world suggested by the sound itself. There is the world created by the artwork. And then there is the world that exists inside the listener.

These worlds do not have to be identical. In fact, they probably never are. The image I had in mind while creating a track can be completely different from what another person imagines while listening to it. It may actually be one of the most interesting properties of music. A piece of music does not have to dictate an image. It can leave enough space for one to appear.

Clouds, Snow, and Other Imagined Places

This becomes particularly apparent in ‘Cotton Candy Clouds (Remix)’ and ‘There Is No Winter Without Snow’. One moves toward something more abstract and dreamlike, while the other came from a much simpler personal association: the idea that winter, at least in my own imagination, is inseparable from snow. Neither concept required a complicated narrative. Sometimes a musical idea begins with nothing more than an image. A landscape. A season. A movement. A memory. A particular atmosphere.

Music then becomes a way of translating that image into something that does not use images at all. That is one of the paradoxes of electronic music. It is created from machines, software, synthesisers, samples, effects, and technical decisions, yet the final result can still evoke something profoundly physical: a valley, a desert, clouds, snow, wind, distance. The technology disappears behind the perception.

The Pressure of Time

And then there is ‘Last Minute’. Its energy comes from something much less peaceful: urgency. The track was inspired by the relentless rush of everyday life, and its fast, high-energy character reflects that idea. At 138 BPM, it moves differently from some of the more atmospheric moments of the album. It is almost the opposite of drifting. Drifting suggests allowing movement to happen. Last minute suggests having no time left. Perhaps that contrast is another reason the track belongs on the album.

Human experience is rarely consistent. We move slowly and quickly. We remember and forget. We seek peace and create pressure. We return to the past while constantly moving toward the future. An album does not have to resolve these contradictions. It can simply contain them.

After the Release

At the time of its release, Drifting Worlds was simply an album made up of ten individual tracks. Each one had its own origin, its own atmosphere, and its own reason for existing. Together, however, they gradually became something more than a collection of separate pieces. A year later, I can see the album differently.

I can see it as a kind of map. Not a geographical map, but an emotional and imaginative one. Across its different sounds and atmospheres, there are traces of nature, movement, heat, emptiness, love, memory, destruction, abstraction, winter, clouds, and urgency. Some ideas seem to look backward, others forward. Some remain almost entirely within a particular atmosphere, while others move between them. The album does not stay in one place. It drifts. And perhaps that is what the title was trying to say all along.

When New Music Becomes Memory

There is something inevitable about the aging of music. The moment a new track is released, it becomes the realization of its creator’s creative vision. It reaches other people, and eventually, the circumstances of its creation become less important than the fact that the music simply exists. For the artist, however, an album can remain connected to a particular period of creative life. It preserves certain sounds, ideas, decisions, and moods. In that sense, it becomes a kind of timestamp.

That is perhaps what I appreciate most about looking back at ‘Drifting Worlds’ now. It does not need to be new anymore. It simply needs to remain what it was: a record of a particular period of my creative life, created independently and built from ten pieces that happened to occupy different worlds. A year later, the release moment has disappeared. The album has become part of the archive. But the music remains. And perhaps this is the point at which an album becomes something else. Not a new release, but a memory that can still be entered. A world that can still be visited. And sometimes, even after the moment has passed, we can still drift back into it.
