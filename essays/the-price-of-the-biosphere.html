The Price of the Biosphere

Jayface • Published: 2026. September 14.

Industrial Expansion and the Desacralization of Nature

When examining the cultural, spiritual, and technological evolution of humanity, the ultimate victim of modernity is not merely human creativity or aesthetics. The most severe and irreversible destruction caused by the profit-maximizing and technocratic world order affects the earthly ecosystem itself - the biosphere. The pressure of industrialization and linear economic growth not only grinds down humanity and culture but also views nature itself as a systemic obstacle. The ongoing global pollution of air, water, and soil, along with desertification and invasive agricultural technologies (such as genetic modification and the use of synthetic chemicals), can contribute to the creation of artificial environments that increasingly displace and disrupt organic life on the planet.

The Critique of Anthropocentrism and Deep Ecology

The roots of the ecological crisis of modern civilization were illuminated by the Norwegian philosopher Arne Naess through his concept of deep ecology. Naess pointed out that mainstream thinking is driven by a fatally flawed and shallow human-centeredness (anthropocentrism), which treats nature exclusively as a free resource for human consumption and profit - a kind of warehouse inventory.

In contrast, deep ecology holds that every element of the biosphere - from microscopic microorganisms and pollinating organisms to global water systems - has intrinsic value, and its right to exist is independent of human utility. When modern industry dismantles biodiversity and turns beautiful, organic landscapes into unlivable, sterile zones, it is actually dismantling the biological foundations of life itself.

Technological Exploitation: Nature as a Standing Reserve

This global process can be understood as one of the most destructive material manifestations of the technological mindset examined by Martin Heidegger. Heidegger argued that the essence of modern technology lies in the “challenging” and forcing of nature: the earth is no longer approached primarily as the source of organic growth and nourishment, but increasingly as a system to be optimized, controlled, and compelled to produce maximum yields through technological and chemical intervention.

The large-scale application of genetic modification and the mass production of highly processed foods can be seen as manifestations of this mindset. A 1970s film already offers an interesting cultural parallel. What appeared absurd and comical in the 1970s increasingly resembles reality today - and what was once comedy might soon become a dystopia? The technocratic mindset does not cooperate with nature. The time required for organic development - as we have previously seen in the workings of cultural industries - is rejected by the market here as well: the goal is immediate, synthetic results, regardless of whether this may lead to long-term ecological collapse.

The Dystopia of a Silent Spring

One of the possible ecological consequences of this process was already anticipated by biologist Rachel Carson in her groundbreaking work, Silent Spring. Carson demonstrated scientifically how chemical pollution can invisibly and step by step disrupt food chains, and warned that if ecological balances are severely disrupted, a time could come when the sounds of nature fall silent forever. Therefore, the protection of nature and respect for organic life should by now be a fundamental interest of every living being. Nature is the most precious foundation of life; if it ceases to exist, so does life itself.
