The Emptying of the Landscape

Jayface • Published: 2026. September 23.

What Does It Mean When a Landscape That Once Felt Full of Life Begins to Feel Strangely Empty?

The rhythmic chirping that resonates during summer days and well into the late hours of the night is a fundamental part of our collective memory. For many of us, these sounds have long served as the natural background of the season: the crickets, bush-crickets, and grasshoppers filling the evenings with a presence that is easy to overlook precisely because it has always seemed so natural. Yet in recent years, I have increasingly found myself wondering whether something in this familiar soundscape is changing. Meadows, fields, forest edges, gardens, and vineyards that once seemed vibrant with life can sometimes feel strangely different. It is not merely the fading of a familiar sound that is unsettling, but the impression that something once present within these landscapes is gradually disappearing.

Crickets, bush-crickets (katydids), and grasshoppers are far more than aesthetic ornaments of the landscape. Their sounds are among the most recognizable expressions of summer, but their presence also represents a much larger and largely invisible world of life moving through grasses, vegetation, soil, and trees. We rarely see most of these creatures, yet their presence is made perceptible through the sounds they produce. When those sounds become less familiar, we are confronted with something that is otherwise difficult to perceive: the possibility that the living texture of the landscape itself is becoming thinner.

Perhaps this is why the idea of silence does not quite describe what is happening. Silence is simply the absence of sound. What I find more disturbing is the sense of emptiness that can remain behind it. A landscape may still look almost exactly as it did before. The fields are still there, the trees are still standing, the roads and vineyards still follow their familiar paths. Yet the experience of that landscape can change when the countless small forms of life that once inhabited it become less present.

A Chemical Filter from the Ground to the Canopy

One of the most visible aspects of modern land management is the increasing effort to control the landscape. Vegetation is managed, unwanted plants are removed, insects are controlled, and agricultural spaces are often organized according to a high degree of predictability and efficiency. Herbicides, insecticides, mechanical soil disruption, and other forms of intensive management can transform the vegetation that insects depend upon, reducing the variety of plants and structures that once provided shelter and food.

This transformation is particularly noticeable in intensively managed agricultural landscapes, including vineyards and orchards. When the vegetation between rows is removed, the ground becomes increasingly uniform. What may appear to us as a cleaner or more orderly landscape can simultaneously become a poorer habitat for many forms of life.

The same principle can extend beyond cultivated land. Hedgerows, woodland edges, shrubs, and other transitional habitats provide countless small spaces in which insects can live, feed, reproduce, and hide. When these structures are reduced or altered, the landscape may lose not only individual species but also some of its complexity. And perhaps this is where the idea of emptying becomes important. A landscape does not have to be physically destroyed in order to lose some of its life. It can remain visually intact while gradually becoming less inhabited.

Soil Drought: The Invisible Demographic Trap

Another important part of this transformation is the increasing dryness of the landscape. Prolonged periods of drought can affect grasslands, woodland edges, agricultural land, and the vegetation growing between vineyard rows. For insects whose life cycles are closely connected to plants and soil, changes in moisture can alter the conditions in which they develop and survive.

The ground is not merely an inert surface beneath our feet. It is part of the living system of the landscape. Beneath the grass and vegetation exists another world that remains almost completely invisible to us: eggs, larvae, microorganisms, roots, fungi, and countless other organisms whose existence depends upon particular environmental conditions.

When the soil becomes excessively dry and vegetation struggles under prolonged heat, the effects are not necessarily limited to what we can immediately see. The loss of moisture can contribute to a chain of changes in which plants become less abundant or less suitable, habitats become less hospitable, and the small organisms dependent upon them become increasingly vulnerable. This is another reason why environmental change can be so difficult to perceive. We tend to notice what happens above the surface, while much of the transformation takes place beyond our immediate field of vision.

The Chain Reaction

The disappearance of insects is not simply about insects. Their presence forms part of a much larger network of relationships. They are eaten by birds, amphibians, reptiles, mammals, and other insects, and their existence contributes to the movement of energy through the wider ecosystem. When one layer of this living network becomes diminished, the consequences can extend beyond the species that first disappeared.

Yet there is another dimension to this loss that is more difficult to measure. When a field becomes quieter, we do not immediately think about food chains or ecological networks. We simply notice that the evening feels different. The landscape seems less animated. The familiar sounds that once surrounded us have become less prominent, and with them disappears some of the feeling that the landscape is inhabited by an enormous number of living beings beyond our immediate perception. This is why the loss of insect life can be understood not only as an ecological issue, but also as a loss of experience. Something changes in the way a place feels when the life within it becomes less audible and less visible.

What Do Science and Art Say?

The decline of insects has increasingly attracted scientific attention, but the feeling that something is being lost from the natural world is not exclusively a scientific concern. Long before contemporary ecological discussions, artists and writers were already attentive to the relationship between human beings and the smaller forms of life surrounding them.

Dave Goulson, in *Silent Earth*, writes about the consequences of the continuing decline of insect populations and the profound dependence of our world upon these often overlooked organisms. His work points toward a reality that is easy to forget: the smallest forms of life can play roles far greater than their size would suggest. John Keats expressed a different dimension of the same idea in his famous poem *On the Grasshopper and Cricket*.

He wrote: “The poetry of earth is never dead…”.

For Keats, the sounds of these small creatures were not merely background noise. They belonged to the living expression of the Earth itself. The grasshopper and the cricket became part of a larger poetic continuity that persists beyond human activity. Perhaps this is why the disappearance of their voices can feel like more than an ecological statistic. When the sounds of insects fade, we lose not only something within the food web, but also something within the sensory and cultural experience of the landscape. We lose one of the ways in which the landscape speaks to us.

Conclusion

Perhaps, then, the most important question is not simply why summer evenings are becoming quieter. It is what that quietness reveals. A landscape can remain green. It can remain beautiful. It can remain productive and visually intact. Yet it may still lose some of the fullness that once made it feel alive. This fullness was never created by a single species. It emerged from countless interactions between plants, insects, birds, animals, microorganisms, soil, water, and climate. Most of these interactions remained invisible to us. We simply experienced their combined result as a landscape that felt full of life.

When parts of that living complexity disappear, we may not immediately recognize what has changed. There may be no dramatic moment of destruction, no clearly defined boundary between a living landscape and an empty one. Instead, the transformation may reveal itself slowly through memory. We remember how summer used to sound. We remember the fields, the evenings, the vegetation, the constant movement of small creatures. And one day we realize that something is missing. Perhaps this is the most unsettling form of environmental change: not the sudden destruction of something we clearly recognize as valuable, but the gradual disappearance of things whose importance we understood only after they had begun to fade.

The landscape does not necessarily become silent. It becomes less full. And perhaps the real question is not simply: Where have all the sounds gone? But rather: What does it mean when a landscape that once felt full of life begins to feel strangely empty?
