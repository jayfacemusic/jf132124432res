The Emptying of the Sky

Jayface • Published: 2026. October 2.

The Global Decline of Aerial Insectivores

When we read about the global biodiversity crisis, the news most often draws our attention to the destruction of tropical rainforests, the melting of polar ice caps, or the disappearance of species from distant and seemingly remote parts of the world. There is, however, another quieter phenomenon taking place directly in front of us - or more precisely, above our heads.

Across many parts of the Northern Hemisphere, one of the most familiar features of summer landscapes has been changing. The swallows that once seemed almost inseparable from villages, fields, farms, rivers, and city skies are becoming less numerous. The swift silhouettes cutting rapidly across the evening sky, the sudden turns of swallows above meadows, and the constant movement of birds hunting insects in the air are becoming less common in many places.

Ecologists often refer to this group of birds collectively as aerial insectivores. They include swallows, martins, swifts, and flycatchers - species whose lives are closely tied to the abundance of insects in the air. Across different regions of the world, many of these birds have experienced substantial population declines, making them one of the more visible signs of broader ecological change.

A Global Pattern Taking Shape

The decline is not confined to a single country or landscape. Long-term monitoring has documented significant decreases among aerial insectivores in North America, while many European bird populations have also come under increasing pressure from agricultural intensification, habitat loss, and changes in land use. In Canada, for example, long-term monitoring indicates that aerial insectivore populations have declined substantially since the 1970s, with the group experiencing one of the most pronounced long-term declines among Canadian birds.

Similar concerns have emerged elsewhere. In Europe, populations of many insect-eating birds associated with agricultural landscapes have declined alongside the intensification and transformation of those landscapes. In parts of Asia, monitoring of migratory birds has also documented declines in species that depend heavily on flying insects. The details differ from one region to another. The species are not all declining at the same rate, and the causes are not identical everywhere. Yet the broader pattern is difficult to ignore: in many places, the sky is carrying less life than it once did. Why are these birds particularly vulnerable? Part of the answer lies in the very thing that makes them so extraordinary.

The Disappearance of the Flying Biomass

Aerial insectivores have evolved to feed on insects while in flight. Their lives are therefore intimately connected to the invisible abundance of insects moving through the air. When that abundance changes, the consequences can reach much further than the insects themselves. Across many regions, declines in insect abundance and diversity have been documented and associated with factors such as intensive agriculture, pesticide use, habitat loss, and urbanisation. The picture is complex and varies between ecosystems, but for birds that depend heavily on flying insects, a reduction in available prey can create an increasingly difficult environment in which to survive and reproduce.

The problem is particularly acute during the breeding season. A nest full of hungry chicks requires a constant supply of food. When insects become harder to find, parents may have to travel farther and work harder to provide enough nourishment. Under some conditions, reduced food availability can affect breeding success and the survival of young birds. What appears to us as an empty patch of air may therefore represent something much more fundamental: an emptying food web. The birds are not disappearing from an unchanged world. They are disappearing from a world in which the conditions that once supported them are changing.

The Phenological Mismatch

Climate change introduces another, more subtle problem. Biological systems are governed by timing. Migratory birds return, insects emerge, plants flower, and young animals are raised according to seasonal cycles that have evolved over very long periods of time. But these cycles do not necessarily respond to environmental change at the same speed.

Spring temperatures are changing, and the seasonal development of insects can shift accordingly. Migratory birds, however, may not always adjust their arrival and breeding schedules at exactly the same rate. In some ecosystems, this can create what ecologists call a phenological mismatch - a growing separation between the time when birds need food and the period when their insect prey is most abundant. The result is not necessarily dramatic or immediately visible. There is no single moment when the system suddenly breaks.

Instead, the timing begins to drift. A bird arrives slightly too late. An insect population reaches its peak slightly earlier. A breeding season begins while the abundance of suitable prey is already changing. Small differences in timing can accumulate into larger ecological consequences. The problem is not simply that spring is changing. It is that the different components of an ecosystem may no longer be changing together.

A Built Environment With Less Room for Life

The natural environment is not the only thing that has changed. The human-built environment has changed as well. Swallows and other species have lived alongside people for centuries, often finding unexpected opportunities for nesting in places such as the eaves of buildings, bridges, barns, farm structures, and other constructions that were never intentionally designed for wildlife. As buildings become increasingly sealed, controlled, and separated from their surrounding environment, some of these accidental opportunities disappear.

This does not mean that modern architecture itself is responsible for the decline of these birds. The relationship is more subtle than that. Rather, it reflects a broader change in how we construct and manage the spaces around us: fewer cavities, fewer sheltered surfaces, fewer rough edges, fewer places where wildlife can simply exist without having been deliberately accommodated. Even something as ordinary as access to mud can become difficult in highly urbanised landscapes. Swallows use mud to construct their nests, yet natural sources of suitable mud may become scarce where soil is sealed beneath concrete, asphalt, and carefully maintained surfaces. We have become remarkably efficient at creating environments that work for us. But efficiency often leaves little room for anything that was not included in the original design.

What Happens When the Sky Becomes Quieter?

The decline of aerial insectivores is not merely the story of a few bird species. These birds occupy a particular position within the food web. Their presence reflects the availability of insects, suitable breeding conditions, functioning habitats, and the seasonal rhythms that connect different parts of an ecosystem. Their disappearance can therefore be understood as a signal. When the birds that feed on insects begin to disappear, something may already be changing beneath them. And the consequences can extend beyond the birds themselves. A reduction in natural insect predation may alter ecological relationships and, in some circumstances, contribute to changes in populations of insects that affect agriculture or human health. The exact consequences vary between ecosystems, but the principle remains important: removing one part of a food web rarely leaves the rest of it completely unchanged.

Nature is not a collection of isolated species. It is a network of relationships. And perhaps this is why the disappearance of swallows and swifts can feel so strangely difficult to describe. Their absence does not necessarily announce itself. There is no broken tree, no empty field, no dead forest visible from a distance. There is simply less movement. Less sound. Fewer dark shapes crossing the evening sky. Perhaps one of the most unsettling aspects of ecological decline is that we often notice it only after the world has already changed.

We remember how a summer once looked without ever consciously recording it. We remember the swallows appearing above the rooftops, the sudden movements over fields, the sound of birds somewhere overhead. We remember these things as ordinary because they were ordinary. Until they are not. The emptying of the landscape does not stop at the ground. It continues upward. And when the sky begins to empty as well, perhaps we should not ask only where the birds have gone. We should ask what has happened to the world that once made their presence possible.
